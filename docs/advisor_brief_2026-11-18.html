All verification suites pass; the decentralisation construction is confirmed numerically for the first time; every theorem in §§6–8 now has a measured residual. What to take away

1. §§6–8 — every theorem — had no experimental support. They now do: nine equilibrium identities hold at solver tolerance (worst residual 2.0×10−10 on quantities of order 105), and two sign/normalisation errors in the manuscript separate from the corrected versions by twelve orders of magnitude.

2. Tables 1 and 3 report error bars larger than the effects they surround. The comparison is paired by construction. Reported as paired, every effect is significant and wins 30/30; the unpaired interval is up to 23.8× wider.

3. The §9.2 claim that value grows with renewable share does not reproduce in welfare (factor 0.96 across a 15%→75% sweep) but does reproduce, strongly and monotonically, in prices: the day-ahead risk premium grows 16.4× over the same sweep. The claim should be restated in the currency where it holds.

4. Correction to revision 1 of this memo. The out-of-sample experiment (E4) is not a true holdout. Train and test draw from the same 92-day pool and overlap in 98.9% of days. It tests resampling robustness, not generalisation. Details in §II.4.

I.2   Fleet selection — and a labelling caveat the paper must not inherit

The instance uses 6 solar buses, 6 wind buses and 6 thermal buses, plus one aggregate load. Renewable buses are ranked by mean output at the delivery hour, not annual mean: the top solar bus at 03:00 is meaningless. Thermal units are aggregated by bus, the six largest buses are kept, and the aggregate unit's marginal cost is the capacity-weighted average of its constituent units.

Caveat — 270 of 280 heat rates were floored

The dataset's gas heat rates are not physical: mean 4.02, min 0.62, max 5.28 MMBtu/MWh, against ~6.4 for a real combined-cycle unit and ~10 for a combustion turbine. Each unit's heat rate is therefore floored at a technology minimum. Count of units floored: 224 of 224 gas, 43 of 53 coal, 3 of 3 nuclear = 270 of 280.

Consequence worth stating in the paper: because every gas unit hits the same floor, the entire gas fleet has an identical marginal cost of exactly $21.20/MWh. The merit order inside the gas block is flat, which is one source of LP degeneracy. A strictly increasing tie-break of +1e-3 × i is added to every unit's cost to keep the per-agent incidence numbers from being arbitrary. Setting heat_rate_floor=False recovers the raw values.

I.4   What a “scenario” is — the single most important modelling choice

The predecessor took 500 consecutive hours as 500 scenarios. That conflates the diurnal cycle with forecast uncertainty: system solar runs 0 → 13.8 → 0 GW within one day, so such an instance asks “what time is it?” rather than “what will the wind do at 18:00 tomorrow?” Here a scenario set is built by conditioning on a delivery hour.

STEP 1
8,760 hourly rows
One non-leap year of nodal solar, wind and load
STEP 2
Condition
hour-of-day == 18 and month ∈ {6,7,8}
STEP 3
Pool: 92 days
Every summer day's 18:00, and nothing else
STEP 4
Draw S = 500
rng.choice(pool, 500, replace=True)
STEP 5
Equal probabilities
ρω = 1/500 for all ω

The spread across scenarios is therefore genuine cross-day uncertainty for a fixed delivery period, which is what the two-stage formulation is about.

Caveat — S = 500 draws, but only 92 distinct realisations

The summer pool is 30 + 31 + 31 = 92 days; the winter pool is 90. Drawing 500 scenarios with replacement from 92 days means every distinct day appears about 5.4 times. Verified for the headline draw (seed 2025): 92 distinct hours, each appearing 1–10 times, mean multiplicity 5.4.

Three things follow, and all three should be in the paper:

  • The effective sample size is 92, not 500. Any standard error computed as if there were 500 independent scenarios is too small by roughly √5.4 ≈ 2.3.
  • The β=0.95 tail is about 5 distinct days. 26 tail scenarios at multiplicity 5.4 is roughly five underlying summer evenings. The CVaR is an average over five days.
  • It is the reason E4 is not a true holdout (§II.4).

The fix is not more draws; it is more pool. Either widen the conditioning window (summer + shoulder months, or ±1 hour around the delivery hour), or obtain more than one weather year. |Ω| and the distinct-day count both need stating explicitly — the draft currently states neither anywhere.

I.5   Sizing: why the load level is rescaled

Full WECC load (mean 82 GW, peak 141 GW) dwarfs any sub-selected six-bus fleet. An instance built from the raw levels sits in permanent deep scarcity: the price pins to VOLL in every scenario and the clearing rule stops mattering. So the load level is rescaled by a single constant so that firm capacity carries peak load with a 15% planning reserve margin, which is how operational test systems are normally sized:

load_scale = ( firm_capacity / (1 + reserve_margin) ) / peak_load
           = ( 36,732.79 / 1.15 ) / peak      ->  0.2363  (summer 18:00)

The load shape across scenarios — the thing the uncertainty lives in — is untouched. The scale differs per configuration (0.2363 summer 18h, 0.3193 summer 8h, 0.3274 winter 8h, 0.3259 winter 18h) because each has a different peak. In the E3 decarbonisation sweep the scale is frozen at the base value so that the sweep varies only the generation mix.

I.6   Recourse and the deviation bid prices

Day-ahead quantities (g, d) are fixed by the clearing problem; the realised loss in scenario ω is then evaluated under optimal recourse:

l(w) = sum_i [ mc_i G_i(w) + a_i |G_i(w) - g_i| ]
     + sum_j [ -mv_j D_j(w) + a_j |D_j(w) - d_j| ]
  s.t.  0 <= G(w) <= gbar(w),  0 <= D(w) <= dbar(w),
        sum_j D_j(w) - sum_j d_j  ==  sum_i G_i(w) - sum_i g_i

Scenarios decouple once the day-ahead commitment is fixed, so minimising the unweighted sum minimises each scenario independently. The deviation bid prices of Eq. (3) are set as αi = mci × δ and αj = VOLL × δ with δ = 0.1 by default. E6 sweeps δ precisely because this constant was previously hard-coded with the comment “just a heuristic”.

Used for every identity check (D1–D5, T1–T8, CVaR pricing). 10 generators, 1 load, S = 500, seed 2025. It is designed so that the tail is sharp and the agents are heterogeneous by construction — on a real fleet with scenario-invariant thermal capacity, the obstruction of §8.2.2 would be numerically invisible.

Two design features matter for the theory. First, the shock is common-mode: it hits all six unreliable units together, so scenarios in the tail are genuine system events, not independent unit failures. Second, demand rises in exactly the states where supply falls, so the tail is scarcity from both sides. That is what produces an Eq. (94) violation large enough to measure (§II.8, D2).

Without Arrow–Debreu securities Eq. (94) fails and the risk-aware allocation is not decentralisable. At ε = 0 every agent's γ is zero, so (94) holds trivially: an energy-only market does support the risk-neutral allocation. “Completing the insurance market is worth 5.5% of system cost” is the same number stated honestly, and it is a result worth reporting. This substitution is valid because unserved energy is identical under both policies (verified to relative 4.7×10−8), so E[Σd̄] cancels.

Eq. (94) without insurance, on this fleet

Each agent is re-solved facing the same prices but with no insurance market:

  • Planner tail scenarios: 26 of 500 (expected (1−β)S = 25)
  • Agents' tail scenario counts: min 25, max 25
  • maxk |γk − γ| = 0.0120, against a requirement of 0

Honest note on the narration. The log prints “the agents do not agree on where the tail is”, but on this instance every agent's tail count is 25 against the planner's 26 — the counts nearly coincide. What establishes the Eq. (94) failure here is the multiplier deviation (0.012), not the counts. The counts genuinely spread on the synthetic instance (21–29, §II.8). That hard-coded sentence should be softened in the code and must not be repeated in the paper for the IM3 case.

II.2   E2 — who insures whom

New · supports Remarks 8 and 9, which the draft states without measurement

At equilibrium the insurance price is q = ρ̃, so the premium an agent pays in excess of its expected payout is Σω(qω − ρω) rkω. Under the corrected Eq. (111)–(112) this splits exactly into an agent-specific term and a flat pool share:

OWN RISK    pi_k  = sum_w (q_w - rho_w) L_k(w)        agent-specific
POOL SHARE       = -(1/N) sum_w (q_w - rho_w) l(w)    identical for all agents

The arbitrary constants δk drop out of both, so πk is the one incidence measure that does not depend on a modelling choice.

Two caveats belong in the text alongside this table:

  • πk is exactly zero for the coal- and nuclear-labelled buses. Thermal capacity in thermal_gens.csv is constant across scenarios, so an infra-marginal unit has constant loss L and Σω(q−ρ)L = L·0 = 0. The gas rows are non-zero only because those buses are marginal in some scenarios. Modelling forced outages would give thermal genuine tail exposure; that is the obvious next experiment.
  • The Eq. (111) equal-share rule hides the signal. The flat pool share is −$104,273/h and swamps the per-agent term, so the “equal-share net” column is nearly identical across generators regardless of their actual exposure. A contribution-weighted δk would allocate it sensibly. That is a property of the rule, not of the market, and is worth a remark.

II.3   E3 — decarbonisation: the claim fails in welfare and holds in prices

New · §9.2 and Appendix C — needs a decision before submission

§9.2 states that “the value of risk-aware clearing becomes more pronounced as exposure to renewable uncertainty grows.” The sweep holds total installed capacity and the load level fixed and varies only the mix, so thermal retires as renewables enter — which is what decarbonisation actually is, and what puts weight in the tail. Adding renewables on top of an unchanged thermal fleet, which is what the draft's experiment does, leaves firm capacity covering peak load and the tail untouched.

The new finding — and it rescues the claim

The welfare value is flat (factor 0.96, slightly down). But the day-ahead risk premium p0RA − p0RN grows monotonically and steeply: −1.16 → −1.73 → −4.19 → −6.28 → −19.05 $/MWh, a factor of 16.4, with no reversals. The risk-neutral price itself jumps 2.3× at the top of the sweep (21.82 → 50.97) as firm capacity falls below mean load and the system enters genuine scarcity.

So the mechanism §9.2 describes is real and visible — it just does not show up in the welfare metric the draft chose. Restate the claim in prices: “as the firm fleet retires, the risk-aware day-ahead price departs ever further from the risk-neutral one,” which is both true here and more interesting, because it is the market signal a regulator would actually observe.

Why welfare stays flat is worth one sentence of its own: the value gap is dominated by deviation cost, which scales with δ (§II.6), not with scarcity depth. Scarcity changes who is marginal and therefore the price; it does not change the adjustment bill by much.

Note finally that in the draft this claim rests on gaps of +0.0038 to +0.0102 against error bars of ±0.0064 to ±0.0246 — entirely inside the noise. It was never supported either way.

II.4   E4 — out of sample, and an important correction

Revised · revision 1 of this memo overstated what this experiment shows

The obvious referee objection to any CVaR result is that CVaR fits the tail it was shown. The experiment clears on one scenario set and evaluates the resulting day-ahead commitment on a second set drawn from the same conditional pool, over 30 replications with paired seeds.

 Risk-aware minus risk-neutral; negative means risk-aware is better. 30 replications, train S=400 (seeds 1000…1029), test S=400 (seeds 500000…500029). The tail gain is 4.18× the expected-value loss — the trade the theory predicts.
Correction — this is not a true out-of-sample test

Train and test use different seeds, so they are different draws. But both draw from the same 92-day summer pool (§I.4), and at S=400 each draw covers essentially all of it. Measured over the 30 replications:

fraction of TEST days that also appear in TRAIN
    mean 0.9894    min 0.9670    max 1.0000

98.9% of the test days were already seen during clearing. So E4 establishes that the risk-aware commitment is robust to re-weighting the same 92 days — a real and non-trivial property, since it rules out the result being an artefact of one particular empirical probability vector — but it does not establish generalisation to unseen weather. Revision 1 of this memo described it as the number that “distinguishes CVaR genuinely reduces tail loss from CVaR fits the in-sample tail”. It does not, and that sentence should not go in the paper.

The fix is cheap and must be run before submission: split the 92 days into two disjoint day sets (46/46) and resample within each, or condition on a wider window so the pool is large enough to partition. Either way the claim becomes defensible. Flag E4 as a resampling check until then.

II.9   T1–T8 and the CVaR pricing invariants

Run at S=200 (theory) and S=100 (pricing) across ε ∈ {0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 1.0}. Every check passes at all levels. The two that matter most:

The pricing suite quantifies the damage: using the old denominator changes E[Π] by −8.14, −21.03, −31.76 $/MWh at ε = 0.1, 0.3, 0.5, with a maximum per-scenario relative change of exactly ε (10%, 30%, 50%).

II.10   Two theory gaps found

T11 · Eq. (107) presumes ε < 1

At ε = 1 the tilted measure concentrates entirely on the tail, so ρ̃ω = 0 off it and pω = νω/ρ̃ω is a genuine 0/0 — real-time prices do not exist off the tail. Measured: the support collapses to exactly 5 of 100 scenarios, which is (1−β)S, and pω is finite on all 5. The valid range is ε ∈ (0, 1), exactly where ρ̃ and ρ are mutually absolutely continuous. This is a companion to the existing ε > 0 hypothesis and needs one sentence in §8.5.1. It is also why the D5 table has em-dashes at ε=1 — those must render as “—” with a footnote, never as nan.

T10 · the program is bounded only because α− > 0

Found while debugging the tail-welfare suite. The vectorised model in tail_model.py imposes a day-ahead nameplate cap g ≤ maxω ḡω, d ≤ maxω d̄ω that is not in the paper's Eq. (63)–(65) — those bound only the realised quantity x + x+ − x−. Removing the cap makes the LP unbounded for zero-marginal-cost renewables: with mc = 0 the deviation prices mc·δ are also zero, so the day-ahead quantity carries zero objective weight and is pinned only by Σd = Σg — a free ray. Gurobi fails outright (SolverError, job 9283311).

The paper should state that boundedness of the day-ahead stage requires α− > 0 strictly, and that a nameplate cap is the natural way to make it structural rather than parametric. Logged in full, including two wrong diagnoses along the way, in docs/EXPERIMENT_LOG.md.

Part IV  ·  Limitations to state, and one thing to fix

Must be fixed before submission

E4 needs a day-disjoint split (§II.4). Everything else here can be stated as a limitation; this one is a claim that does not currently hold as described. It is a small change — partition the 92-day pool — and the suite reruns in minutes.

Should be stated plainly in the text

  • Unserved energy is identical under all policies. Generation capacity is exogenous and VOLL exceeds every marginal cost, so the planner always serves the physical maximum; unserved energy agrees to relative 4.7×10−8 across risk-neutral, risk-aware and the intermediate policies. Risk-aware clearing reallocates adjustment cost; it cannot improve adequacy. Saying so immediately after the tail results pre-empts the question rather than inviting it, and it bounds the scope of the claim rather than weakening it.
  • Effective sample size is 92, not 500 (§I.4). State |Ω| and the distinct-day count.
  • 270 of 280 heat rates were floored (§I.3), and the entire gas fleet therefore shares one marginal cost.
  • The load level is rescaled by a constant to a 15% reserve margin (§I.5). The shape is untouched, but the absolute $/h figures are not WECC-scale.
  • E2's rows are bus labels, not fuel labels (§I.2).
  • Thermal has no scenario-dependent capacity, hence structurally zero tail premium. Forced-outage modelling is the obvious extension.
  • E1, E2, E3, E6 and the seasonal table are single-seed — no error bars. Only E4 and E5 are replicated.
